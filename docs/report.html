What it takes to label rare traffic incidents from camera data

A weekend test on 21,885 real frames from LTA's 90 expressway cameras. Lim Zi Qiang.

Question

Incident labels are rare. Can a cheap pipeline find them in camera data: rank frames with signals that need no labels, let a vision-language model check the candidates, and confirm by hand?

Result

1 of 235 frames I labelled showed an incident. The model flagged 32; none held up.

Each tile is one labelled frame. Filled red: incident. Red outline: the model said incident.

  • The cameras didn't see the crashes. None of the 9 crashes reported within 1 km of a camera was visible 1 and 10 minutes after the report (0 of 28 frames): they were out of the camera's view, not between snapshots. The random sample had none either (0 of 80; 95% interval 0 to 4.6%).
  • An off-the-shelf model over-flags. Qwen3-VL 32B flagged 32 frames, none correct (95% interval 0 to 11%), and missed the one real incident. It read motion blur as an overturned car, brake lights as stopped vehicles and checkpoint queues as incidents.
  • The response shows even when the crash doesn't. In three reported crashes, later frames show police, SCDF or recovery vehicles with lights on in the resulting queue.

What I would build instead

The ranking and the model both need work, but the first problem is coverage: the cameras see a small part of the road, and most incidents happen out of their view. So turn the order around:

  1. Find where and when to look from a signal that covers every road, all the time: speed bands, loop detectors, the incident feed.
  2. Pull 30 to 60 seconds of video from the nearest cameras rather than single stills; stopped vehicles and responders are clear in motion and ambiguous in one frame.
  3. Measure the model's precision on a small labelled set before trusting it, and point it at the cues seen here: vehicles that don't move, and emergency or recovery vehicles.
  4. Send the rest to a person; confirmed labels retrain the cheap ranker, so each pass needs fewer human checks.

Method

  • Frames. LTA has published live images for only 8 cameras since mid-2026; data.gov.sg keeps the history of all 90. For 10 crashes reported in the news (Feb 2025 to May 2026): every camera every 5 minutes from 20 minutes before to 40 minutes after, plus the same hour a week earlier as a control.
  • Rankings. Image novelty (CLIP embedding distance to the same camera's other frames, ignoring those within 45 minutes), CLIP zero-shot similarity to incident descriptions, and both combined. The top 50 camera-time windows from each, 80 random frames and 28 frames at the reported crashes formed a shuffled review set of 235. The one incident was ranked #28 by image novelty.
  • Labels. One labeller (me), blind to rankings and model output. Incident: a crash, a stopped or broken-down vehicle, debris, responders attending, or people on the carriageway; congestion alone is not.
  • Model. Qwen3-VL 32B via NUS SoC's API, temperature 0, fixed JSON reply with an abstain option, about 6 s per frame.

Limits

  • 235 frames and 10 crashes: enough to show the pattern, not to rank models precisely.
  • One labeller; single frames 5 minutes apart; some incidents are only clear on video.
  • One general-purpose model and prompt, untuned: the lesson is to measure before trusting, not that VLMs cannot do this.
  • Public cameras only, one still every few minutes; video would show far more. No speed or loop data: it exists only live.
